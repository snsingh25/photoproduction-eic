\subsection{Comparison with machine-learning taggers}

Deep-learning classifiers built on jet constituents are the benchmark for
quark/gluon discrimination at the
LHC~\cite{Komiske_2018,Metodiev_2018,Kasieczka2017-bu}, so it is fair to ask what
a single physically-motivated observable gives up against them. Two things frame
the comparison. A learned classifier sees all the constituent-level information
and can in principle reach whatever discrimination is available, so it is really
an upper bound rather than a competitor. But reaching that bound in practice
takes calibration, and nobody can yet quote a systematic uncertainty for a
tagger trained on simulation and applied at a facility with no data.

We can make this quantitative because Ref.~\cite{lee2023machine} studies
machine-learning quark/gluon tagging in exactly this final state. That work
generates low-$Q^2$ photoproduction dijets at $\sqrt{s} = 141$~GeV, clusters them
with anti-$k_T$ at $R = 1.0$ within $|\eta_{\mathrm{lab}}| < 4$, labels QQ,
$Q\bar{Q}$ and GG topologies from the generator process codes while dropping
mixed $qg$ final states, and trains three classifiers on the leading-jet
constituents: a Particle Flow Network (PFN) that is given particle-identification
information, an IRC-safe Energy Flow Network (EFN), and a deep network fed
IRC-safe Energy Flow Polynomials (EFPs) of dimension $d=7$. Jet mass serves as
their single-observable baseline. The jet definition, the pseudorapidity
acceptance, the photon virtuality, the centre-of-mass energy and the handling of
mixed topologies all match what we do here. The differences are the generator
(\textsc{Pythia}~6 rather than \textsc{Pythia}~8) and the dijet thresholds
($p_{T1} > 8$, $p_{T2} > 5$, $p_{T3} < 4$~GeV there; $E_{T1} > 10$,
$E_{T2} > 7$~GeV and exactly two jets here).

Reference~\cite{lee2023machine} shows ROC curves but never quotes areas, so we
obtained the values in Table~\ref{tab:ml} by digitising its Fig.~9. The
$\pm 0.015$ is the full spread we get from shifting the axis calibration by
$\pm 3$ pixels and from taking either the mean or the median of the plotted
curve, which has finite width. One more thing to note: their ROC treats the
quark as signal, the opposite of our convention. Swapping the two classes and
flipping the sign of the discriminant leaves the area unchanged, so the numbers
compare directly.

Three things follow, and they are more specific than the usual blanket claim that
jet shapes are ``competitive'' with machine learning.

First, $\Psi(0.3)$ is indistinguishable from the two IRC-safe learned
classifiers. Its AUC of $0.757$ sits between the EFN at $0.760 \pm 0.015$ and the
EFP network at $0.749 \pm 0.015$. A single calorimetric observable with nothing
to train recovers, to the precision of this comparison, everything the deep sets
get out of the full IRC-safe constituent information.

Second, $\Psi(0.3)$ does \emph{not} match the best learned tagger. The PFN with
particle identification reaches $0.788 \pm 0.015$, about $0.03$ ahead, which is
more than the digitisation uncertainty. The reason is not mysterious. The PFN is
the only classifier here that is told what the particles are, and the
charged-hadron and kaon content carries quark/gluon information that a
transverse-energy profile cannot see by construction. So the comparison measures
what you give up by using a calorimetric shape instead of full particle-flow
input, and at these energies the answer is a few percent in AUC.

Third, the margin over a single-observable baseline is large.
Reference~\cite{lee2023machine} uses jet mass in that role and gets
$0.697 \pm 0.015$, which $\Psi(0.3)$ beats by about $0.06$. Physically motivated
observables are not interchangeable, and picking the right one matters as much as
the choice between a physical observable and a learned one.

Put together, these numbers support a narrower claim than is usually made for
traditional observables. Machine learning does keep a measurable edge at the EIC,
but only when it is handed information beyond the energy flow, and the edge is
small enough that a first measurement --- dominated by detector systematics that
neither study includes --- is unlikely to resolve it.
